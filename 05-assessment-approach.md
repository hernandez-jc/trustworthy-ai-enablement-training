# Trustworthy AI Enablement: Assessment Approach

[← Back to AI RMF Learning Orientation](04-ai-rmf-learning-orientation.md) | [Back to README](README.md)

## Purpose

This page describes how learning is assessed within the portfolio.

The assessment approach is designed to determine whether learners can **recognize trustworthy AI considerations, question assumptions, evaluate risks and trade-offs, and make more deliberate decisions about human oversight and responsible GenAI use**.

The emphasis is not on memorizing AI terminology.

The emphasis is on **decision capability**.

---

## Assessment Design Principle

A learner may be able to define terms such as *bias*, *transparency*, *human oversight*, or *monitoring* without being able to apply them to a real decision.

This portfolio therefore assesses learning through **decision-oriented scenarios**.

The learner is presented with a situation in which an AI-enabled workflow creates a meaningful question, tension, or risk.

The learner must then consider:

* What is happening?
* What information is missing?
* What could go wrong?
* Who remains accountable?
* What level of human oversight is appropriate?
* What evidence would increase confidence?
* What boundaries should apply?
* What should be monitored?
* What trade-off is being made?

This creates a stronger connection between **AI literacy and workplace decision-making**.

---

## From Knowledge to Decision Capability

The assessment follows the capability progression introduced in the learning objectives:

**Recognize → Identify → Question → Evaluate → Decide → Monitor**

| Capability    | Assessment Evidence                                                 |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Recognize** | Identifies relevant AI lifecycle or trustworthiness considerations  |
| **Identify**  | Identifies potential risks, accountability gaps, or limitations     |
| **Question**  | Challenges assumptions, missing evidence, or inappropriate reliance |
| **Evaluate**  | Weighs competing considerations and available evidence              |
| **Decide**    | Selects an appropriate management or oversight response             |
| **Monitor**   | Identifies what should be observed or reviewed over time            |

The progression does not imply that every learning activity must measure all six capabilities.

Instead, it provides a design reference for determining **how far a scenario should ask the learner to reason**.

---

## Scenario-Based Assessment Model

A representative assessment sequence can follow this structure:

```text
Scenario
   ↓
Identify the decision
   ↓
Surface the AI-related concern
   ↓
Examine evidence and uncertainty
   ↓
Consider human oversight
   ↓
Evaluate trade-offs
   ↓
Select a response
   ↓
Determine what should be monitored
```

The sequence can be adapted according to the complexity of the learning objective.

A shorter scenario may assess recognition and identification.

A more complex scenario can require evaluation, decision-making, and monitoring considerations.

## What Is Being Assessed?

### 1. Recognition of Trustworthiness Considerations

Learners should be able to recognize when an AI-enabled workflow raises questions related to characteristics such as:

* validity and reliability
* safety
* security and resilience
* accountability and transparency
* explainability and interpretability
* privacy enhancement
* fairness and harmful bias

The objective is not to require learners to become technical specialists in each characteristic.

The objective is to help them recognize **when a characteristic may matter to a decision**.

---

### 2. Quality of Human Oversight

Assessment can examine whether learners distinguish between:

* nominal human involvement
* meaningful human review
* appropriate escalation
* inappropriate automation
* excessive reliance on AI output

A human being present in a workflow does not automatically mean that meaningful oversight exists.

The assessment therefore asks learners to consider whether the person responsible has:

* sufficient information
* sufficient authority
* sufficient context
* an opportunity to question the output
* an appropriate point of intervention

---

### 3. Accountability

Scenarios can assess whether learners recognize situations in which accountability becomes unclear.

For example:

> An AI system produces a recommendation, a human accepts it, and the resulting decision creates a problem. Who was responsible for the decision, and what evidence would help establish how it was made?

The assessment does not attempt to provide a legal determination.

Instead, it develops the learner's ability to identify **accountability gaps that require clarification or management attention**.

---

### 4. Responsible GenAI Boundaries

Generative AI introduces additional questions because outputs can appear useful, plausible, or confident without necessarily being appropriate for the intended use.

Assessment can therefore ask learners to determine:

* what the system may appropriately be used for
* what should remain subject to human review
* what information should not be provided
* when verification is required
* when escalation is appropriate
* when a use case should be restricted or reconsidered

The goal is to move from:

> "Can we use GenAI for this?"

toward:

> "Under what conditions can this use be responsible and appropriately controlled?"

---

### 5. Monitoring

A decision about AI use is not necessarily complete when a workflow is deployed.

Assessment should therefore include situations in which learners must consider what needs to be monitored over time.

Depending on the scenario, this may include:

* unexpected outputs
* changes in performance
* emerging patterns of harmful bias
* inappropriate reliance on generated content
* changes in the operating context
* user behavior
* escalation patterns
* evidence that existing controls are no longer sufficient

This reinforces the idea that responsible AI use includes **ongoing attention rather than one-time approval**.

---

## Assessment Through Trade-Offs

Trustworthy AI decisions rarely involve a single consideration.

A scenario may deliberately create tension between:

* efficiency and confidence
* automation and human control
* productivity and verification
* personalization and privacy
* speed and review
* consistency and fairness
* explainability and operational complexity

The learner is therefore not always expected to find a single universally correct answer.

Instead, the assessment can evaluate whether the learner:

1. identifies the relevant considerations
2. recognizes uncertainty
3. distinguishes assumptions from evidence
4. considers affected stakeholders
5. evaluates the level of human oversight
6. identifies an appropriate boundary or control
7. explains the reasoning behind the decision

This makes **reasoning quality** more important than simple answer selection.

## Example Assessment Pattern

### Scenario

A team introduces a GenAI assistant to summarize internal information and prepare recommendations for managers.

The outputs appear useful and save time.

After several weeks, however, a manager notices that some recommendations are based on incomplete information.

### Assessment Questions

**Question 1 — Recognition**

What is the primary concern?

* The tool is not fast enough.
* The output may be unreliable or incomplete.
* The organization is using too much technology.
* Employees need more general AI awareness.

**Question 2 — Human Oversight**

What would represent meaningful human oversight?

* Accept every output because the system has already been approved.
* Require a human to review recommendations before consequential decisions.
* Remove humans from the workflow to improve efficiency.
* Ask users to trust the system unless an obvious error appears.

**Question 3 — Boundary**

What is the most appropriate next question?

> Under what conditions should the assistant be allowed to generate recommendations, and when should human review or escalation be required?

**Question 4 — Monitoring**

What should the organization monitor after implementation?

Possible considerations include:

* reliability of outputs
* recurring errors or omissions
* inappropriate reliance
* changes in the workflow or context
* user escalation patterns
* evidence that existing controls are insufficient

**Question 5 — Decision Rationale**

What evidence would increase confidence that the workflow is being used responsibly?

This final question moves the assessment beyond recognition toward **evaluation and decision-making**.

## AI RMF-Informed Assessment Lens

The assessment approach can use the four AI RMF functions as a conceptual lens.

| Function    | Assessment Question                                                                   |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **GOVERN**  | Who has responsibility, authority, and oversight?                                     |
| **MAP**     | What is the context, intended use, affected population, and potential impact?         |
| **MEASURE** | What evidence can be used to evaluate performance, risk, or trustworthiness?          |
| **MANAGE**  | What action, boundary, mitigation, escalation, or monitoring response is appropriate? |

These functions are not treated as four isolated quiz categories.

They provide a way to structure the learner's reasoning across a scenario.

---

## Assessment Feedback

Feedback should explain **why** an answer is stronger or weaker rather than simply marking it correct or incorrect.

A useful feedback pattern is:

**Decision → Reasoning → Risk → Better Question**

For example:

> **Decision:** The recommendation should receive human review before it informs a consequential decision.
>
> **Reasoning:** The workflow produces useful outputs, but the scenario provides evidence of incomplete information.
>
> **Risk:** Accepting the output without review could create inappropriate reliance on the system.
>
> **Better Question:** What evidence and review conditions are necessary before this type of recommendation can be used?

This approach reinforces transferable reasoning rather than answer memorization.

---

## What This Assessment Does Not Do

This portfolio assessment approach does **not** claim to:

* certify AI governance competence
* conduct an organizational AI risk assessment
* audit an AI system
* validate the technical performance of an AI model
* determine regulatory compliance
* provide legal advice
* replace specialist risk, security, privacy, legal, or technical evaluation
* reproduce an official NIST assessment methodology

The purpose is educational:

> **to develop and assess the learner's ability to recognize trustworthy AI considerations and make more informed decisions about AI-enabled workflows.**

---

## Design Principle

Effective AI enablement should not stop at explaining what AI is.

It should help people **decide how AI should be used, where human judgment remains necessary, what boundaries should apply, and what needs to be monitored after adoption**.

The assessment therefore measures a progression from **AI awareness toward responsible decision capability**.
