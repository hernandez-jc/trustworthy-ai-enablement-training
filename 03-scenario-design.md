# Trustworthy AI Enablement: Scenario Design

[← Back to README](README.md)

## Purpose

This page describes how scenario-based learning is used to help managers and decision makers examine **trustworthiness considerations, trade-offs, human oversight, and responsible GenAI adoption**.

The scenarios are intentionally designed around decisions rather than definitions.

The learner is placed in a situation where an AI-enabled capability may offer a meaningful benefit, while also creating questions that require judgment.

The objective is not to teach learners to identify a single predetermined answer.

It is to develop the ability to **recognize relevant considerations, question assumptions, evaluate trade-offs, and determine what responsible action may require**.

---

## Why Scenarios?

AI-related risks are often difficult to understand when presented only as abstract concepts.

A definition of algorithmic bias, transparency, or accountability does not necessarily prepare a manager to recognize how those issues could affect a real decision.

A scenario creates a bridge between:

**Concept → Context → Decision → Consequence → Reflection**

This is particularly useful for AI enablement because the learner may need to make decisions despite incomplete information.

The training therefore treats uncertainty as part of the learning experience.

---

## Scenario Design Principle

Each scenario is built around a **decision tension**.

The basic design logic is:

```text
AI capability
      ↓
Business or operational value
      ↓
Trustworthiness consideration
      ↓
Decision tension
      ↓
Managerial judgment
      ↓
Human oversight / boundary
      ↓
Monitoring or follow-up
```

The scenario should make the learner ask:

> **What would responsible use require in this context?**

rather than:

> **What is the correct definition of this risk?**

---

## Scenario Components

A representative scenario contains several elements:

| Component                         | Learning purpose                                                |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Context**                       | Establishes the business or operational situation               |
| **AI use case**                   | Clarifies what the AI capability is being used to do            |
| **Decision point**                | Gives the learner a meaningful management decision              |
| **Trustworthiness consideration** | Introduces a relevant concern                                   |
| **Trade-off**                     | Creates tension between competing considerations                |
| **Learner judgment**              | Requires a decision or recommended response                     |
| **AI RMF-informed reflection**    | Connects the decision to risk-management concepts               |
| **Management implication**        | Reinforces accountability, oversight, boundaries, or monitoring |

The components are intentionally lightweight.

The portfolio demonstrates the **design logic** without publishing a complete facilitation manual or reusable consulting playbook.

# Representative Scenario 1: The Uneven Recommendation

## Context

A business unit is considering an AI-supported recommendation system to help prioritize cases for human review.

Initial testing suggests that the system could reduce the time required to identify cases requiring attention.

However, early observations indicate that outcomes may differ across groups represented in the data.

The proposed users are interested in expanding the system because of the expected efficiency benefit.

---

## Decision Point

The manager must decide whether the proposed use should:

* proceed as planned;
* remain restricted while additional evaluation is conducted;
* require additional human review;
* or be reconsidered for the intended purpose.

---

## Trustworthiness Consideration

The scenario introduces questions related to:

* fairness and harmful bias;
* validity and reliability;
* affected stakeholders;
* evidence required to support the proposed use;
* accountability for decisions influenced by the recommendation.

The scenario does not assume that the presence of an observed difference automatically establishes a particular conclusion.

The learner must consider **context, evidence, potential impact, and intended use**.

---

## Trade-Off

**Efficiency vs. confidence in the proposed use**

The organization may gain operational efficiency, but expanding the system without understanding the observed differences could introduce additional risk.

The learner must determine what information or action is appropriate before relying more heavily on the system.

---

## Learner Decision

The learner is asked:

> **What should the manager consider before expanding the use of the AI-supported recommendation system?**

The learner should identify:

* the relevant context;
* who may be affected;
* what evidence is needed;
* whether additional evaluation is warranted;
* the role of human review;
* and who remains accountable for the resulting decisions.

---

## AI RMF-Informed Reflection

The scenario can be examined through several AI RMF functions:

**MAP**

What is the intended use, who may be affected, and what potential impacts require consideration?

**MEASURE**

What evidence or evaluation would help the organization understand the observed outcomes?

**MANAGE**

What response may be appropriate given the current level of understanding and potential impact?

**GOVERN**

Who is responsible for deciding whether the system should be expanded or restricted?

---

## Learning Takeaway

A useful AI capability is not automatically an appropriate capability for every intended use.

The decision should consider **context, evidence, potential impact, and accountability** rather than efficiency alone.

# Representative Scenario 2: The Plausible GenAI Output

## Context

A department introduces a Generative AI workflow to help employees prepare first drafts of customer-facing material.

The workflow produces outputs that are generally useful and significantly reduces drafting time.

Over time, however, employees begin treating the generated material as reliable without consistently verifying it.

Some inaccurate outputs reach later stages of the workflow before being detected.

---

## Decision Point

The manager must determine whether to:

* continue the workflow as currently used;
* establish stronger review expectations;
* restrict the type of material that can be generated;
* introduce additional escalation or verification requirements;
* or reconsider the workflow for certain uses.

---

## Trustworthiness Consideration

The scenario introduces questions related to:

* reliability;
* human oversight;
* accountability;
* transparency about AI involvement;
* appropriate reliance on generated content;
* monitoring of actual use.

The issue is not simply whether the GenAI system can produce useful content.

The issue is whether the **workflow surrounding the system supports responsible use**.

---

## Trade-Off

**Productivity vs. appropriate reliance**

The organization is receiving a clear efficiency benefit.

At the same time, increasing reliance on AI-generated output may create a gap between the level of human review expected and the level of review actually taking place.

---

## Learner Decision

The learner is asked:

> **What should the manager change, clarify, or monitor before allowing the workflow to continue at its current level of use?**

The learner considers:

* the intended use;
* the consequences of inaccurate output;
* the level of human review required;
* user behavior;
* accountability;
* escalation;
* and ongoing monitoring.

---

## AI RMF-Informed Reflection

**GOVERN**

Who establishes the expectations for appropriate use and review?

**MAP**

What types of content and decisions are being supported, and who could be affected?

**MEASURE**

What information would help determine whether the workflow is functioning as intended?

**MANAGE**

What changes to use, oversight, or boundaries may be appropriate?

---

## Learning Takeaway

Responsible GenAI adoption concerns more than the quality of individual outputs.

It also concerns **how people use, review, rely on, and make decisions based on those outputs**.

# Representative Scenario 3: The Accountability Gap

## Context

An organization uses an AI system to support a high-impact business decision.

The system produces a recommendation that is normally reviewed by an employee.

Over time, the employee's role shifts toward confirming the system's recommendation rather than independently evaluating it.

When an unexpected outcome occurs, different stakeholders disagree about who was responsible for the final decision.

---

## Decision Point

The manager must determine whether the current workflow provides sufficient accountability and meaningful human oversight.

Possible responses may include:

* clarifying decision authority;
* strengthening human review;
* defining escalation conditions;
* restricting the system's role;
* or reassessing the workflow before further use.

---

## Trustworthiness Consideration

The scenario focuses on:

* accountability;
* transparency;
* human oversight;
* decision authority;
* organizational responsibility.

The central issue is not whether the AI system technically produced a recommendation.

The issue is whether the surrounding workflow preserves **meaningful human responsibility for the decision**.

---

## Trade-Off

**Automation efficiency vs. meaningful human control**

The organization may have introduced the AI system to make the process faster and more consistent.

However, efficiency can create a new risk if the human role becomes largely symbolic.

---

## Learner Decision

The learner is asked:

> **Where should responsibility and decision authority remain, and what evidence would demonstrate that human oversight is meaningful?**

The learner considers:

* who owns the decision;
* whether the reviewer has authority to challenge the recommendation;
* whether the reviewer has sufficient information and context;
* when escalation should occur;
* what should happen when the AI recommendation is unsuitable;
* and whether the current workflow needs to be changed.

---

## AI RMF-Informed Reflection

**GOVERN**

Are roles, responsibilities, and accountability sufficiently clear?

**MAP**

What is the decision context and who may be affected?

**MEASURE**

What evidence could demonstrate whether the human-control mechanism is functioning as intended?

**MANAGE**

What response is appropriate if the current arrangement creates an accountability gap?

---

## Learning Takeaway

A workflow does not necessarily provide meaningful human oversight simply because a person remains somewhere in the process.

The quality of oversight depends on whether the responsible person can **understand, question, challenge, and act on the AI-supported output when appropriate**.

# Cross-Scenario Learning Pattern

Although the scenarios involve different concerns, they follow a common learning pattern.

| Scenario               | Primary concern                      | Central trade-off                     | Decision capability                                                            |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Uneven Recommendation  | Fairness / harmful bias and evidence | Efficiency vs. confidence             | Recognize potential impact and determine what evidence or action may be needed |
| Plausible GenAI Output | Reliability and human oversight      | Productivity vs. appropriate reliance | Establish boundaries and review expectations                                   |
| Accountability Gap     | Accountability and human oversight   | Automation vs. human control          | Clarify responsibility and meaningful decision authority                       |

The scenarios deliberately overlap.

Trustworthiness considerations rarely occur independently in real AI-enabled workflows.

For example, a decision involving potential bias may also raise questions about:

* accountability;
* transparency;
* reliability;
* human oversight;
* monitoring;
* and organizational governance.

The learning design therefore avoids treating trustworthiness characteristics as isolated categories.

---

# From Scenario to Decision

The scenarios are intended to develop a repeatable **thinking pattern**, not a prescriptive checklist.

Learners are encouraged to move through questions such as:

```text
What is the intended use?
        ↓
What is the context?
        ↓
Who may be affected?
        ↓
What could go wrong?
        ↓
What evidence do we have?
        ↓
What remains uncertain?
        ↓
Where must human judgment remain?
        ↓
What boundary or response is appropriate?
        ↓
What should be monitored?
        ↓
When should the decision be revisited?
```

This pattern supports AI fluency without requiring the learner to become a technical AI practitioner.

---

# Facilitator Design Consideration

The facilitator can use the scenarios to probe learner reasoning rather than immediately provide a preferred answer.

Useful discussion prompts include:

* **What makes this decision difficult?**
* **What information would change your decision?**
* **Who could be affected by this choice?**
* **Who is accountable?**
* **Where could human oversight become ineffective?**
* **What would you want to monitor?**
* **What boundary would you establish?**
* **What would cause you to revisit the decision?**

The purpose is to surface the learner's reasoning and assumptions.

This supports an active learning experience without prescribing one universal response to every scenario.

---

# Design Boundary

The scenarios in this repository are **representative learning prototypes**.

They are not presented as:

* real client cases;
* evidence of organizational implementation;
* legal or regulatory case analysis;
* technical AI evaluations;
* or universal examples of how an organization should respond to a specific risk.

Their purpose is to demonstrate how AI enablement training can create opportunities for **risk-aware decision practice**.

---

# Portfolio Evidence

This scenario design demonstrates the ability to translate complex AI topics into **managerial learning experiences**.

The design combines:

* scenario-based learning;
* decision practice;
* trustworthiness considerations;
* AI RMF-informed vocabulary;
* human oversight;
* GenAI boundaries;
* risk-aware adoption;
* reflection and discussion;
* and ongoing monitoring.

The scenarios also demonstrate an important AI enablement principle:

> **The learning objective is not to make managers technical AI specialists. It is to improve the quality of the decisions they make around AI-enabled work.**
