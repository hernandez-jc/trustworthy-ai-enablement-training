# Trustworthy AI Enablement: Learning Objectives

[← Back to README](README.md)

## Purpose

This page defines the learning outcomes for the training prototype.

The objectives are designed around a practical question:

> **What should a manager or decision maker be able to recognize, question, evaluate, and decide after completing the learning experience?**

The emphasis is on **applied AI fluency and decision capability**, rather than technical AI development skills.

---

## Overall Learning Goal

Enable managers and decision makers to make more informed decisions about the **adoption, use, oversight, and ongoing monitoring of AI-enabled workflows** by recognizing relevant trustworthiness considerations, accountability requirements, and opportunities for human judgment.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the learning experience, participants should be able to:

### 1. Recognize the AI Lifecycle

**Recognize** that AI-related risk and trustworthiness considerations can arise across the lifecycle, including design, development, deployment, use, and monitoring.

Learners should understand that deployment is not necessarily the end of the decision-making process.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can identify where a scenario presents a lifecycle-related decision or monitoring need.

---

### 2. Identify Trustworthiness Considerations

**Identify** relevant trustworthiness considerations in practical AI scenarios.

These may include:

* validity and reliability;
* fairness and harmful bias;
* accountability and transparency;
* explainability and interpretability;
* privacy;
* security and resilience;
* safety.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can identify which considerations may be relevant to the context rather than applying a generic risk label to every AI use case.

---

### 3. Recognize Accountability Gaps

**Recognize** situations in which responsibility for an AI-supported decision, output, or workflow may be unclear.

Learners consider:

* who is responsible for the decision;
* who has authority to approve or reject an AI-supported outcome;
* what happens when an AI recommendation is incorrect;
* whether human involvement represents meaningful oversight.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can identify an accountability gap and articulate why it matters to the proposed use.

---

### 4. Apply an AI RMF-Informed Vocabulary

**Use** the concepts of **GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE** to structure questions about an AI use case.

The learner is not expected to reproduce the NIST AI RMF.

Instead, the functions provide a practical vocabulary for considering:

**GOVERN**
Roles, responsibilities, organizational expectations, and accountability.

**MAP**
Context, intended use, affected stakeholders, potential impacts, and risks.

**MEASURE**
Evidence, evaluation, performance, and monitoring considerations.

**MANAGE**
Risk response, prioritization, oversight, and decisions about how risks should be addressed.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can use the appropriate function or combination of functions to frame questions about a scenario.

---

### 5. Evaluate Human Oversight

**Evaluate** whether a proposed AI-enabled workflow provides an appropriate role for human judgment.

Learners consider whether people responsible for reviewing or using AI outputs have:

* sufficient context;
* an opportunity to question the output;
* authority to intervene;
* appropriate escalation options;
* clear responsibility for the resulting decision.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can distinguish meaningful human oversight from nominal human approval.

---

### 6. Establish Responsible GenAI Boundaries

**Determine** appropriate boundaries for a proposed Generative AI use case based on its context, intended purpose, potential impacts, and level of reliance.

Possible decisions may include:

* proceeding with defined conditions;
* restricting the intended use;
* requiring additional review;
* gathering further evidence;
* escalating the decision;
* or reconsidering the proposed use.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can justify a boundary using relevant contextual and risk considerations rather than relying solely on the perceived usefulness of the technology.

---

### 7. Recognize the Need for Monitoring

**Recognize** why AI-enabled workflows may require ongoing monitoring after deployment.

Learners consider whether changes in:

* system behavior;
* use patterns;
* performance;
* context;
* risks;
* or organizational expectations

could require a previous decision to be revisited.

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can identify what should remain visible to responsible stakeholders after an AI capability is introduced.

---

### 8. Evaluate Trade-Offs

**Evaluate** competing considerations when making an AI adoption decision.

Examples include:

| Trade-off                                  | Decision question                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Efficiency vs. oversight                   | How much human review is appropriate for the intended use?                |
| Automation vs. human control               | Which decisions should remain with people?                                |
| Speed vs. evidence                         | Is there enough information to expand the use?                            |
| Usefulness vs. transparency                | Is the available understanding of the system sufficient for this context? |
| Personalization vs. fairness               | Could the intended benefit create uneven impacts?                         |
| Experimentation vs. boundaries             | What conditions should apply while the use case is being explored?        |
| Initial performance vs. ongoing monitoring | What could change after deployment?                                       |

**Evidence of learning:**

The learner can articulate a trade-off and explain how it influences the decision.

---

## Capability Progression

The objectives are designed to move learners from **recognition toward informed action**.

```text id="9x3v9k"
RECOGNIZE
AI lifecycle + trustworthiness considerations
        ↓
IDENTIFY
Risks, impacts, accountability gaps
        ↓
QUESTION
Context, evidence, oversight, boundaries
        ↓
EVALUATE
Trade-offs and available information
        ↓
DECIDE
Adopt, restrict, review, escalate, or reconsider
        ↓
MONITOR
Observe use and revisit decisions
```

This progression reflects the practical nature of the training.

The learner is not expected to become an AI risk specialist. The intended capability is to become **better prepared to participate in responsible AI decisions**.

| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Recognize the AI lifecycle              | Lifecycle awareness          |
| Identify trustworthiness considerations | Risk awareness               |
| Recognize accountability gaps           | Accountability awareness     |

| Recognize monitoring needs              | Ongoing risk awareness       |
| Evaluate trade-offs                     | Decision-making              |

Together, these objectives connect **AI fluency with practical management judgment**.

## Scenario-Based Evidence of Learning

The objectives are designed to be assessed through decisions rather than terminology recall.

A scenario may ask the learner to:

1. identify the relevant concern;
2. determine what additional information may be needed;
3. consider affected stakeholders;
4. identify where accountability resides;
5. evaluate the role of human oversight;
6. consider relevant evidence or monitoring;
7. select or propose a management response;
8. explain the reasoning behind the decision.

The quality of the response is therefore considered in relation to the **reasoning behind the decision**, not simply whether the learner selects a predetermined answer.

---

## What Successful Learning Looks Like

A successful learner should be able to move from:

> **“The AI tool appears useful.”**

toward questions such as:

> **“Useful for whom, in what context, under what conditions, with what evidence, with whose oversight, and with what ongoing accountability?”**

This shift is central to the training approach.

It represents a move from **tool awareness to responsible adoption judgment**.

---

## Relationship to the AI RMF

The objectives are informed by the AI RMF's emphasis on managing AI risks throughout the lifecycle and incorporating trustworthiness considerations into AI-related activities.

The four functions provide an organizing vocabulary:

**GOVERN → MAP → MEASURE → MANAGE**

They are used here to support learning and discussion rather than to create a prescriptive implementation sequence.

The project does not claim that completing this training makes a learner proficient in implementing the NIST AI RMF.

---

## Assessment Implications

Because the objectives focus on judgment, the assessment approach should emphasize:

* scenario interpretation;
* identification of relevant considerations;
* quality of reasoning;
* recognition of uncertainty;
* appropriate use of human oversight;
* boundary-setting;
* risk-aware decision making;
* and recognition of monitoring needs.

This provides the foundation for the assessment approach developed in [`05-assessment-approach.md`](05-assessment-approach.md).

## Portfolio Evidence

This page demonstrates the ability to translate a broad AI enablement goal into **specific, observable learning capabilities**.

It demonstrates:

* outcome-oriented training design;
* alignment between business need and learning objectives;
* AI fluency development;
* risk-aware learning design;
* decision-making skill development;
* scenario-based assessment thinking;
* human oversight awareness;
* responsible GenAI adoption learning.

The objectives deliberately remain at the **managerial and decision-making level** rather than expanding into technical AI development or implementation.

---

## Scope

These objectives describe a **training design prototype**.

They do not establish professional certification, technical competence, regulatory competence, or formal qualification in AI risk management.

The NIST AI RMF is used as an informing reference for terminology and conceptual orientation.
